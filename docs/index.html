01 / Title & Abstract

Can a model tell editors which pages to review first — using only what they'd know in advance?

Editors choose which pages to refresh each month by gut feel, so this project asks whether a ranked queue can pick the pages that are about to lose more than 20% of their search impressions. It uses FlyRank's anonymized content-refresh dataset: 30,000 pages from 32 clients, of which 21,578 pages have enough traffic for a decline to be meaningful. A logistic regression sees only information from before the outcome month and is evaluated on 25 client-grouped folds, against the base rate, a staleness rule, and the hand-built rule from earlier weeks. On clients it never trained on, it raised precision@20 from a 0.63 base rate to 0.83 (± 0.09), while the staleness rule and the hand-built W04 rule ranked no better than chance (AUC 0.49 and 0.52). The lift is concentrated at the top of the list (overall AUC ≈ 0.62), so the output is a per-client top-10 review queue with reason codes — decision support for where to look first, not a traffic forecast or proof that refreshing works.

02 / Introduction

The decision this work supports

An editor opens a client's site with time to refresh maybe ten pages this month, out of hundreds. Individual numbers are visible — page age, clicks, search demand — but nothing turns them into an ordered list. So the pick defaults to "oldest first" or "lowest traffic first", and nobody checks afterwards whether those were the right pages.

This paper treats the task as ranking, not forecasting. The editor doesn't need next month's exact traffic; they need the top of the list to be pages that genuinely are about to slide. A wrong pick costs an editor hour on a healthy page; a miss lets a page keep losing visibility.

How the project got here

The path matters, because two earlier "results" turned out not to hold:

  • W02: a hand-weighted staleness + clicks + demand score lost to staleness alone (precision@20 0.29 vs 0.31). Kept as the finding: hand-picked weights aren't reliable here.
  • W04: a transparent rule — stale and CTR below half its position-tier benchmark.
  • W05: a random forest beat that rule on one held-out set of 6 clients (0.600 vs 0.300).
  • W06: the validation audit found the W05 number rested on one small split and on features that overlapped the outcome's own time window. This paper reports the corrected design.

What was excluded, and why

  • Pages under 30 impressions in the comparison month. In W05, all three most-confident errors were pages with 1–3 impressions in 90 days: going from 1 impression to 0 counts as a "decline". That's noise, not a signal.
  • trend_direction, trend_pct — the label is computed from them.
  • Every 90-day aggregate (CTR, average position, engagement, scroll) and update dates — they overlap the outcome window (Methodology).
  • Client and content IDs — used only to group the split. Generation metadata (LLM provider/model) — not a property of the page's search performance.

No client names, domains, URLs or search queries appear anywhere in this paper or the repository; IDs are pseudonyms.

04 / Methodology

Methodology

Label

A page is declining when its impressions in days 1–30 are more than 20% below days 31–60 (trend_direction == "down" in the dataset), on measurable pages only. It is a proxy: seasonality, SERP layout changes or tracking gaps can produce the same drop.

The key assumption: decide before the outcome

A forecast an editor can act on may only use information that existed before the outcome month started. So the model is trained as if the decision were made 30 days ago:

The W05 features — 90-day CTR, average position, impressions, clicks, engagement, and the last-update date — all include days 1–30. A page that dropped in that month also shows a worse 90-day position and CTR, and 68% of pages show an "update" inside that month. Those features describe the decline rather than anticipate it. With one snapshot, this also means position and engagement drop out of the model entirely: the CSV has no earlier sub-window for them.

Features (23, all from days 31–90 or static)

Log impressions, clicks and sessions for each of the two pre-outcome months; impression momentum between them; 60-day CTR; content age; word count; keyword search volume, competition and CPC with explicit "has data" flags (missingness follows content type, so a blind fill would smuggle the type in); content type; search intent.

Validation design

  • Client-grouped cross-validation: GroupKFold by client, 5 folds × 5 shuffles = 25 held-out client groups. No client appears on both sides of a fold (asserted in code). Every candidate is scored on the identical folds.
  • Metrics: precision@20 (primary since W01), precision@50, precision@10 within each client's own queue (how an editor actually uses it), and AUC — always next to the fold's base rate.
  • Model selection, fixed in advance: logistic regression vs random forest on the honest features; the higher mean precision@20 ships.

Leakage checks

  • Assertion that no label-source, ID or generation-metadata column is a feature.
  • Window-overlap audit (above) — found and fixed.
  • Planted leak: adding impressions_last_30d (the outcome itself) sends precision@20 to 1.000. If it hadn't, the evaluation harness would be broken.
  • Random row split reported next to the grouped split, to measure how much "remembering clients" was inflating scores.

  • The selected model matches or beats the W04 rule on 96% of folds, staleness on 84%, and its own fold's base rate on 100%.
  • In an editor's terms: of the top 10 pages in a client's queue, about 7–8 actually declined, versus about 6 for random picks.
  • The overlapping-window model's extra ~0.04 AUC is roughly what reading the outcome window was worth. Giving it up is the cost of a queue that can actually be used ahead of time.
Negative results, kept Staleness — the default "refresh the oldest pages" heuristic — and the W04 stale-AND-low-CTR rule carry no ranking signal on unseen clients (AUC 0.49 and 0.52). Most update dates in this data are two batch values (20 and 104 days), not editorial history. The idea that pages which just rose will fall back (momentum) also shows nothing (AUC 0.48).

3. What the model leans on

Two directional observations, not causes. Visible-but-unclicked pages are at higher risk — the CTR intuition behind the W04 rule survives, as a continuous signal rather than a hard threshold. Younger pages are at higher risk than older ones, the opposite of "stale pages decay". In this slice, declining pages had a median age of 216 days vs 291 for growing pages, which also runs against the direction reported in FlyRank's March 2026 study. That study covers a broader population that includes much younger pages, so this is a scoping difference worth testing, not a contradiction.

06 / Limitations & Honest Framing

What this work cannot claim

Everything here is observed in one dataset, measured on held-out clients, and offered as decision support. Nothing here identifies how a search engine ranks pages, and nothing shows that refreshing a page causes it to recover.

  • Observational, one snapshot. Pages weren't refreshed at random, so the data can't measure what a refresh does.
  • Proxy label. A >20% month-over-month impression drop includes seasonality, SERP changes and tracking gaps, not only content decay.
  • Modest discrimination (AUC ≈ 0.62). The lift is real at the top of a queue; below that, the order means little.
  • Large variation between client groups. Precision@20 std ≈ 0.09 and base rates vary by ± 0.10 across folds. Some clients' queues will be much weaker than the average.
  • The age assumption is the weakest link. Live pages are 30 days older than training pages (drift index PSI 0.93 on age, by construction), and the live queue is dominated by the youngest pages. The first month of real outcomes is the test.
  • No position, engagement or update-date signal in the honest model: the CSV has no pre-outcome window for them.
  • Starter slice only (30k pages, 32 clients, all ≥ 90 days old).
  • No untouched final holdout. Model selection used the same cross-validation; with two candidates the optimism is small but not zero.
07 / Ranked Recommendations

Action playbook

The live queue (W07) scores 20,128 pages and gives each of 29 clients a top-10 review list — 280 pages: 180 REVIEW_FIRST (highest risk, at least median traffic), 93 WATCH (high risk, little traffic), 7 CTR_CHECK. Every page carries up to two reason codes, built from the model's own per-page contributions so they explain the actual score.

The pattern is concrete and checkable in a minute per page: around 100 days old, hundreds to thousands of monthly impressions, and almost no clicks.

In priority order

  1. Each month, review each client's REVIEW_FIRST pages first — highest decline risk with real traffic at stake.
  2. For VISIBLE_LOW_CLICKS, start with the title and meta description. Impressions without clicks is the cheapest thing to test; check which queries the page is shown for before rewriting the body.
  3. For YOUNGER_PAGE, check whether rankings are still settling before a full rewrite.
  4. Review PROFILE-led and WATCH pages last, and re-check them next month.
  5. Stop prioritising by staleness alone. It showed no ranking signal on unseen clients.
  6. Before acting on any page, a person checks: is the drop steady or a one-day dip; is it seasonal; did the query mix change; was the page just edited?
  7. Never automate deleting, noindexing, merging or redirecting from this score; never auto-rewrite and publish; never promise clients traffic outcomes from it; never compare clients by score.
  8. Measure the queue monthly. Record the share of each queue that actually declined. If it falls to the base rate two months running, retire the model; retrain on the newest window every month regardless.
